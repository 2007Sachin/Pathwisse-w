# Pathwisse vocabulary and tone system

**Status:** Working language system  
**Depends on:** `PATHWISSE_WEBSITE_BLUEPRINT.md` and `POSITIONING.md`  
**Purpose:** Define how Pathwisse communicates before final page copy or implementation.

This document governs public website language. It does not turn an unverified capability into a claim. When product scope is uncertain, use plain intent language such as “aims to,” “is designed to,” or “where supported,” or mark the content as illustrative.

## 1. Brand voice

### Clear

**Means:** The reader understands the point without decoding category jargon.  
**Sounds like:** “Know what to work on next.”  
**Does not sound like:** “Optimize your competency acquisition pathway.”  
**Good:** “Connect what you learn to work you can show.”  
**Bad:** “Unlock a holistic pathway to future-ready success.”

### Intelligent

**Means:** Respect the reader’s ability to think; explain the problem precisely.  
**Sounds like:** Specific distinctions between learning, application, evidence, and readiness.  
**Does not sound like:** Dense abstractions or jargon used to appear sophisticated.  
**Good:** “A certificate records completion. A project can show how you approached a problem.”  
**Bad:** “Our capability layer operationalizes multidimensional talent signals.”

### Human

**Means:** Start from the student’s or institution’s real question.  
**Sounds like:** “What should I learn next?” and “Who needs support before placement season?”  
**Does not sound like:** Corporate distance, motivational fluff, or fake intimacy.  
**Good:** “You have learned a lot. Now make the next piece of work count.”  
**Bad:** “We empower every learner to become their best self.”

### Credible

**Means:** Say only what the product, company, or approved evidence supports.  
**Sounds like:** Clear boundaries, illustrative labels, and no invented proof.  
**Does not sound like:** Unqualified outcomes, inflated comparisons, or vague authority.  
**Good:** “Pathwisse is intended to connect development with placement preparation.”  
**Bad:** “Pathwisse guarantees placement success.”

### Ambitious

**Means:** Address a meaningful structural problem without overstating current capability.  
**Sounds like:** “We’re rebuilding the last mile of education: getting hired.”  
**Does not sound like:** Grand claims unsupported by the current product.  
**Good:** “Learning should move toward something you can demonstrate.”  
**Bad:** “We are revolutionizing the global future of work.”

### Calm

**Means:** Confident, spacious, and considered; urgency without fear.  
**Sounds like:** One clear next action and an honest explanation.  
**Does not sound like:** Scarcity, shouting, excessive exclamation marks, or a wall of CTAs.  
**Good:** “Request a conversation about your institution’s context.”  
**Bad:** “Act now before your students fall behind forever.”

## 2. Core language philosophy

- **Clarity before cleverness:** A sentence earns attention by being understood, not by sounding like a slogan.
- **Concrete before abstract:** Prefer a role, task, project, decision, or conversation over “transformation.”
- **Human before corporate:** Use the words a thoughtful student, faculty member, or placement officer would use.
- **Evidence before hype:** Separate what is verified, approved positioning, illustrative, hypothesized, and unverified.
- **Confidence without superiority:** State the intended role firmly; do not claim “best,” “only,” “leading,” or “revolutionary.”
- **Journey before inventory:** Explain how stages connect before listing products or features.
- **Progress before perfection:** Readiness is a developing conversation, not a single promise or score.
- **Specificity before scale:** If a program, workflow, or proof point is not known, say so rather than filling the gap.
- **Plain language before AI language:** Explain the human benefit first; mention AI only when a verified capability requires it.

## 4. Strategic vocabulary tiers

### Tier 1 — Brand language

Employability, capability, evidence, direction, opportunity, journey.

### Tier 2 — Product and service language

Career direction, role expectations, structured development, applied work, readiness, placement preparation, student progress, support needs.

Use “structured learning,” “program,” “assessment,” “mentor,” “dashboard,” or “integration” only where the actual product or offering has been verified.

### Tier 3 — Action language

Explore, understand, choose, build, apply, practice, demonstrate, show, prepare, explain, review, progress.

### Tier 4 — Institution language

Student readiness, employability development, placement preparation, faculty support, mentorship, cohort context, intervention timing, institutional conversation.

Prefer “support needs” to “gaps” when the tone should be human. Use “intervention” only in an operational institutional context.

## 5. Defining “evidence”

### Conceptual meaning

Evidence means work, outputs, projects, demonstrations, or other credible context that helps a student show what they can do.

### Possible product implementation

The product might support creating, capturing, organizing, reviewing, or communicating evidence through projects, assignments, demonstrations, reflections, resumes, or preparation. These are possibilities from the blueprint, not verified capabilities.

### Validation boundary

Before making evidence central to public product claims, validate:

- what artifact types are supported;
- how ownership and contribution are recorded;
- whether feedback or review exists;
- how evidence relates to a role or task;
- what a reviewer can actually inspect; and
- what evidence cannot prove.

### Word choice

- **Evidence:** preferred strategic term; emphasizes context and credibility.
- **Proof:** use sparingly in brand belief or plain-language contrast; it can imply certainty.
- **Demonstration:** use for an observable act or explanation.
- **Portfolio:** use only for a real collection of work; do not imply a full portfolio product.
- **Project:** use for a defined piece of applied work.
- **Applied work:** preferred when emphasizing learning used in context.
- **Capability:** use for the underlying ability, not the artifact itself.

## 6. Defining “employability”

Pathwisse should use employability to mean the developing ability to understand work, build relevant capability, apply it, demonstrate it, and prepare for opportunity.

Employability does **not** mean guaranteed employment, a placement rate, a salary outcome, or a universal measure of a person’s worth. Students should meet the term after a plain explanation. Institutions may encounter it as the strategic category; student pages should often use simpler language such as direction, work, preparation, and opportunity.

## 7. Defining “readiness”

Readiness is always context-dependent:

- **Skill readiness:** preparation to perform or discuss a specific skill; requires a defined skill and evidence model.
- **Role readiness:** preparation relative to the work expected in a target role; requires role expectations.
- **Placement readiness:** preparation for a particular placement process or conversation; requires defined institutional context.
- **Interview readiness:** preparation to explain experience, decisions, contribution, and limitations; does not guarantee an interview result.
- **Career readiness:** broad umbrella language; use only when the surrounding copy makes the meaning concrete.

Do not use these interchangeably. Any scoring, tracking, or measurement claim requires product validation.

## 9. Institution vocabulary

Institution language should be credible, structured, operational, and clear without sounding like enterprise software.

**Better:** “Understand where students are progressing and where additional support may be needed.”  
**Avoid:** “Unlock 360-degree student intelligence with AI-powered analytics.”

Prefer “college or university” when addressing the sector, “institution” when discussing the operating context, and “placement team / TPO” when speaking to that specific stakeholder. Do not imply dashboards, analytics, interventions, cohort tracking, or integrations unless verified.

## 11. AI language

AI must not be the value proposition. State the human benefit first, then describe AI as supporting technology only when the capability is verified.

**Bad:** “AI-powered career recommendations.”  
**Better:** “Understand which roles may align with your interests, skills, and goals.”

Do not say AI chooses a student’s career, replaces faculty or mentors, guarantees a match, or objectively measures readiness unless those claims are explicitly validated and approved.

## 12. Sentence style

- Prefer 8–18 words for most sentences; vary rhythm deliberately.
- Keep paragraphs to 1–4 sentences on marketing pages.
- Keep headlines short enough to scan; one idea per headline.
- Use sentence case for headings and CTAs.
- Use contractions in student-facing copy: “you’re,” “we’re,” “don’t.”
- Use active voice: “Build work you can explain,” not “Work can be built.”
- Use second person for students; use “your institution” only when it feels natural.
- Use “we” sparingly for company belief and method, not as filler.
- Use questions to surface real tension, not as rhetorical decoration.
- Fragments are acceptable in labels and progression steps, not as a substitute for clarity.
- Avoid exclamation marks except in direct quotes approved for use.

## 14. CTA vocabulary

### Student CTAs

- **Primary:** Start Your Journey or Get Started. Select one final default after signup validation.
- **Secondary:** See How Pathwisse Works or Explore Pathwisse.
- **Tertiary:** Explore Programs, Read the Story, Understand the Approach.

### Institution CTAs

- **Primary:** Request a Demo.
- **Secondary:** Explore Pathwisse for Institutions or See How It Works.
- **Tertiary:** Discuss Your Context, Explore the Approach.

One screen should have one visually dominant action, one quiet alternative, and optional text links. Do not give “sign up,” “request demo,” “explore programs,” and “learn more” equal emphasis.
